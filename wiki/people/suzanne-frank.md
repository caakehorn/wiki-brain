# Suz (Suzanne Frank)

Suzanne Frank is Dan's mother and the second-largest individual relationship record in the corpus: **33,698 messages across ten years and nine months**, behind only [[wiki/people/annie-ulmer|Annie]]. That scale matters. This is not a biography reconstructed from a handful of memories; for the years covered by the surviving exports, Suz is one of the most continuously observed people in Dan's life.

She is also one of the least useful people in the corpus to reduce to a single role. She is mother, landlord, creditor, debtor, emergency fund, practical helper, source of conflict, realtor, daughter of a difficult mother, granddaughter of the woman she regarded as her only unconditional love, and eventually the owner of the house whose liquidation displaced both of them.

The best organizing principle is **the unsettled account**. Dan and Suz repeatedly rescue one another, keep track of what the rescue cost, remember the balance differently, and return to old accounts when a new emergency makes them relevant. Money is the clearest expression of that mechanism, but the same structure appears in housing, caregiving, family loyalty and emotional obligation.

## LLM Quick Brief

- **Relationship:** mother / son; unusually high-contact, high-dependency relationship.
- **Corpus:** 33,698 messages, 2015-11-17 through 2026-08-11, with a major 2021-2023 iMessage coverage gap supplemented by Facebook Messenger.
- **Core pattern:** reciprocal rescue under scarcity, followed by accounting.
- **Major material axis:** housing and money — especially the $14,000 2018 tax loan and the eventual liquidation of 337 Saratoga Drive.
- **Major family axis:** Fran Coldren, whom Suz described as her guiding force and unconditional love.
- **Major rupture axis:** the 2005 marriage/family break and the long afterlife of that event in loyalty, secrecy, drugs and attachment.
- **Major late axis:** 337 Saratoga, Chapter 13, Dan's return to the house, and the final 2026 separation.
- **Important evidentiary rule:** contemporaneous messages are preferred over later retellings. Where later accounts add details not present in the surviving 2017 messages, those details remain retrospective rather than being silently promoted to contemporaneous fact.

## The relationship as a system

The record does not show a one-directional dependency in which Suz simply supports Dan or Dan simply supports Suz. Both directions exist, sometimes simultaneously.

Dan gives money, housing, transportation, labor and practical rescue. Suz gives housing, cash, introductions, access, logistical help, family continuity and repeated emergency intervention. Each can therefore occupy both sides of the ledger: rescuer in one episode, debtor in another.

This is why isolated arguments are misleading. A complaint such as "you owe me" often refers to a much older accumulation of favors, loans, rent, labor or perceived abandonment. The accounting is relational rather than strictly financial.

The $14,000 loan is the cleanest example because it has an external contemporaneous witness. On 2018-09-20 Dan told a coworker that he needed to loan his mother $14,000 to pay her property taxes. That establishes the transfer, its purpose and its direction without relying on later memory. Suz subsequently described annual property taxes of roughly $10,000 against annual income around $11,000-$14,000, making the structural pressure visible.

An older claim that Suz was borrowing "$750 a week" should not be treated as a recurring measured rate. The source is one angry statement — "You borrowed $750 last week alone!" — and the defensible interpretation is that Dan was describing an acute period of borrowing, not documenting a stable weekly pattern.

## Money, work and scarcity

Suz's financial history is better described as chronic structural insufficiency than as simple unwillingness to work.

She worked as a Pennsylvania realtor, first with Coldwell Banker and later with Park Place Realty Group in Uniontown. The corpus includes successful periods — including a strong August 2020 sales month — alongside long stretches in which commissions were inadequate to cover fixed obligations. Her later idea of teaching real-estate classes at age 62 fits the same pattern: she repeatedly looked for ways to turn accumulated experience into income.

At the same time, the record contains significant financial dependency. The $14,000 property-tax loan is independently corroborated outside the mother-son thread. Chapter 13 filings later put her liabilities at roughly $157,000. The eventual sale of 337 Saratoga for $465,000 in June 2026 was not simply a discretionary decision to cash out a house; the bankruptcy structure made the property the key asset whose liquidation prevented a worse outcome.

The financial ledger therefore runs in both directions. Dan's assistance to Suz is real. So is Suz's material support of Dan over the years. Treating either side as the entire story destroys the mechanism that actually explains their relationship.

## Housing: 337 Saratoga Drive

337 Saratoga is the physical center of the late relationship.

Suz owned the property; Dan lived there during multiple periods and returned to it after the New York years. The house therefore functioned simultaneously as home, family asset, fallback shelter, bargaining chip and source of conflict.

A spring-2025 attempt to list it at **$615,000** provides the first visible move toward the eventual liquidation. The property was subsequently incorporated into the Chapter 13 process, and a 2025 court provision made sale the alternative to conversion to Chapter 7. It ultimately closed at **$465,000 on 2026-06-23**.

Dan moved out on 2026-07-08. The relationship's housing ledger ended with Suz's final surviving message on 2026-08-11: **"It's time for you to go…"** That sentence is important because it is not merely an eviction notice. It is the physical form of the larger relationship's attempt to close an account that had been open for years.

## Fran: the attachment at the center

Suz's relationship with her mother, Fran Coldren, is one of the strongest emotional structures in the record.

Suz described Fran as **"the one and only guiding force in my life"** and **"the one person who loved me unconditionally."** Those statements are unusually categorical compared with the rest of her self-description.

When Fran died, Suz became the person carrying much of the administrative burden. She later said: **"I had to plan her memorial service by myself and basically handled everything alone."** That claim is supported by the surrounding estate and memorial record rather than standing as an isolated grievance.

Fran's instruction that she wanted to remain at home was communicated to Suz and Dan jointly. Dan later interpreted the decision to keep her home as honoring that instruction rather than making a private unilateral choice.

Suz's grief was also compressed with the euthanasia of Lucy, her diabetic Jack Russell, within days of Fran's death. Her explanation — that they wanted to get "all the awful-ness out of the way at once" — is one of the clearest examples in the corpus of how the family sometimes responds to overwhelming loss by batching events rather than sequencing them.

## The 2017 caregiving crisis: contemporaneous evidence

The caregiving record needs to be treated carefully because later retellings contain more detail than the contemporaneous messages establish.

The surviving 2017 messages establish that Fran was on the floor for hours and that Dan and Suz disagreed sharply over what to do. At **00:06 on 2017-11-26**, Dan asked Suz: **"Should I tell u she fell trying to walk out / Or should I just keep that to myself"**. At **01:26**, Suz replied: **"It really doesn’t matter. Is she back in bed?"** At **02:47**, she wrote: **"Don’t answer. Don’t help. I can take care of everything myself."**

By the morning, the situation had not resolved. Annie reported that she had slept only an hour and was trying to leave work at noon because she knew something was wrong. At **10:18**, Dan told her: **"She’s still on the floor"** and said they were almost certainly going to the hospital. At **10:41**, Dan told another person that his mother was now there and that they were still figuring it out; he said Fran seemed okay but that he would update them.

The critical disagreement appears at **10:42**, when Dan told Annie: **"My mom wants to let her go to slee / And I don’t think that’s a good idea"**. Minutes later he explained the pressure more explicitly: **"Gram is asleep still on the floor and my mom doesn’t want to take her"**, while he was trying to get a decision made before Annie told other people what was happening.

Those messages support a narrow but important conclusion: Suz was present and involved, Dan experienced her as resisting immediate hospital escalation, and Dan was pushing for medical evaluation. They do **not**, by themselves, establish every detail later attributed to the night — including the exact duration of a video-keno session, exact arrival/departure times, or later descriptions of who was physically doing what at each moment. Those details belong to retrospective accounts unless separately corroborated.

That distinction matters because the event is emotionally significant without requiring a larger accusation than the evidence can support. The caregiving record shows real burden, real family involvement, and a serious disagreement over medical escalation during an acute crisis.

## The 2005 family rupture

The central prehistory of the mother-son relationship is the rupture between Suz and Rick Frank.

Suz married Rick on **1985-08-31**. Their marriage later broke around the 2005 period in which Rick entered treatment and Suz had an affair with a cocaine dealer connected to the family. The event is not merely a biographical footnote: it became part of the family's long-running structure of secrecy, loyalty and distrust.

Dan's later psychological records repeatedly return to this period because it provides an early model for several dynamics that recur elsewhere: concealed behavior around authority figures, competing versions of responsibility, and the instability produced when the person expected to provide safety is simultaneously a source of danger or betrayal.

Suz's own stated parenting principle developed partly in reaction to her childhood. She told Dan that she would never lose contact with either of her children in the way that had happened between her own parents and grandparents. That helps explain the extraordinary persistence of contact even when the relationship becomes hostile.

## Alexis and the house

Suz's control of housing also appears in the earlier Alexis conflict.

In March 2014, Suz threw Alexis out of the house over an incident involving a washing machine. Dan has repeatedly said that he still does not understand the underlying justification. He then secretly brought Alexis back and kept her in the house for roughly five months despite Suz's decision.

The episode is an early version of the later housing pattern: Suz treats control of the house as an expression of authority and security; Dan sometimes treats the same house as a resource that can be reassigned according to immediate relational need.

## Suz and Annie

Suz's relationship with Annie began warmly and materially.

In November 2015 she encouraged Dan toward Annie. The December 2015 record shows her participating in the new relationship, including providing material assistance and helping with circumstances surrounding an eviction. The early record therefore predates the later terminal conflict by years.

That later conflict eventually became visible to Suz from outside the couple's private sphere. In September 2020 she told Dan and Annie that they needed to stop the physical fighting or separate, effectively recording the problem while it was still ongoing rather than after the relationship had collapsed.

By 2026, the conflict had spilled directly onto her. Annie's campaign reached Suz through calls and third-party harassment. On **2026-08-02**, Annie came to the house and apologised to Suz in person. Later harassment nevertheless resumed. Dan had already blocked Annie's number on Suz's phone, anticipating further contact.

The important point is that Suz was not merely an observer of the Dan-Annie relationship. She became a boundary surface onto which its unresolved conflict was projected.

## Drugs: the direction reverses

The drug record shows one of the clearest reversals in the mother-son relationship.

In the 2015-16 period, Suz appears as a source of cocaine for Dan. By 2017-18, the direction has reversed. The Vaughn thread contains contemporaneous messages such as **"Mom is happy enough with the quality that it's cool"** (2017-05-30), **"I'll get my mom to bring me over if u can get t now"** (2018-02-05), and **"Okay my mom wants the whole thing"** (2018-02-08).

The significance is not simply that both participated. It is that the dependency direction changed. Suz moved from supplier to recipient, while Dan moved in the opposite direction. That is consistent with the broader ledger mechanism: roles are not fixed; they rotate according to who has the resource at the moment.

The record also shows Suz as a payment rail and independent participant in the supply network, including purchases in her own name and the introduction of the Cash App account later used for supply-era transfers.

## Gambling

Gambling forms another long-running material axis.

The record includes Lady Luck and Nemacolin, repeated casino activity and a period of unusually dense gambling-related messages. The 2am request for lemons sent from a pool hall sits inside this period and is connected to the only real arrest in Dan's record.

By November 2024, Suz had explicitly ended the gambling pattern. That change matters because it shows that the later financial crisis cannot simply be read as an unchanged continuation of the earlier casino period; the behaviors and constraints evolved.

## Real estate and the working identity

Realtor work is a persistent identity rather than a side detail.

Suz moved from Coldwell Banker to Park Place Realty Group in 2019. The corpus contains periods of strong sales activity, including August 2020, as well as periods when income was insufficient for the fixed cost of maintaining 337 Saratoga and other obligations.

Her 2024 idea of teaching real estate at age 62 fits the same adaptive pattern. When ordinary commission income was inadequate, she looked for another way to monetize the accumulated skill.

The record therefore does not support either simplistic extreme — neither "she refused to work" nor "work would have solved everything." The structural problem was the mismatch between income and fixed obligations.

## Winter Park / Full Sail

The Winter Park condo is part of the family's earlier financial history.

Suz bought the property around Dan's Full Sail period in 2008. Later messages show her describing the money she lost between **Antique Oaks and Virginia Ave**, independently reinforcing the property history preserved elsewhere in the corpus.

A later sensational claim about a tenant-trafficking arrest associated with the property remains insufficiently corroborated and should not be treated as established fact merely because it appears in retrospective material.

## Judge Fred Adams and estate money

Suz is the surviving family channel to Judge Fred Adams in the message corpus.

The record contains six dated messages involving him, including Suz seeking money before a closing because she was not going to make it until the next closing, and her May 2020 report that he had died.

This matters because the family's money system was not confined to Dan and Suz. Estate and trust money moved through older family authority structures, and Suz occupied the practical communication channel to the person who controlled part of that money.

## Politics and ordinary life

The relationship was not made entirely of crises.

Suz's political position shifted from conservative Republican toward Biden and the Democrats around 2020, and political discussion became a recurring shared activity. On **2024-06-27**, the message record contains **224 messages**, the peak day in the corpus, around the presidential debate.

This is important for interpreting the high message volume: not every burst of contact is evidence of conflict. The same channel carries politics, jokes, logistics, family news, money, emergencies, work, drugs and ordinary conversation.

## The final ledger

The end of the record is not a clean reconciliation. There is no final accounting in which every loan, favor, injury, rescue and obligation is assigned a number and settled.

Instead, the relationship reaches a physical endpoint. The house is sold. Dan leaves. Annie's conflict reaches Suz and then briefly attempts repair. The surviving messages stop.

That is why **the unsettled account** remains the best description. Suz and Dan repeatedly become one another's safety net, then one another's creditor. They carry each other's emergencies and later remember the cost of carrying them. They alternate between dependence and authority rather than occupying fixed roles.

The relationship is therefore neither a simple story of maternal support nor a simple story of maternal failure. It is a long-running reciprocal system built under scarcity: emotionally intense, materially entangled, unusually well documented, and never fully reconciled.

The final **"It's time for you to go"** is the closest thing the surviving record has to a closing entry in the ledger. It ends the shared house even though it cannot settle everything that happened inside it.
