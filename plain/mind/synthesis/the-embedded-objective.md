# The Embedded Objective

**The short version.** Dan has never stuck with a job because of the job. He
sticks with a private goal of his own that he hides inside the job — and when
that private goal is destroyed, he leaves. Not when the work gets hard, and not
when the boss asks more of him. Where there was no private goal to install, he
lasted weeks.

## The finding, and the prediction it broke

This page exists because the records got something wrong and had to be
corrected.

An earlier entry argued that Dan only finishes things he wants himself, and it
said plainly what would prove it wrong: find a case where he paid a big price,
over a long stretch, for a goal somebody else set him. His work history breaks
it twice over. **41 months at a restaurant. 43 months caddying at a golf
resort.** Against that, the six-month stretch caring for his dying
great-grandmother — the thing the old rule treated as his high-water mark.

So the old rule was wrong. What replaced it is more interesting.

## Five jobs, and what was hiding inside each one

| The commitment | The official job | The private goal inside it | How long |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restaurant | Busser, then server | An equal tip split he talked the veterans into on day one; a fake 9pm night class; sole control of the outdoor shed's cash tips | **41 months** |
| Golf resort | "Experience Associate – Golf" | A caddie status ladder nobody assigned him, plus golf itself as his own sport | **43 months** |
| Gas station | Cashier | **None on record** | **About 1 month** |
| Food courier | App-dispatched driver | **Nothing there to install one in** | 12 months, on and off |
| Court probation | Six-month court-ordered programme | The deal itself — won before the probation started | Only described afterwards |

The first two rows broke the old rule. The last three are what turn the new one
into a real claim instead of a description that fits everything.

## Why the long jobs lasted

The obvious reading is that a 41-month job must have suited him. It didn't.

By his own account the restaurant work was punishing. He is an introvert, and a
freezing Saturday night working fifteen tables took *"literally EVERYTHING i had
in me."* The records name that job as the origin of what he called his "DAN
CYCLE OF JOB ENTROPY." Nothing about the actual work was holding him there.

What held him was a stack of goals that were **his own, private, and often
working against his employer**:

- An equal tip split, negotiated on day one, with no restaurant experience at all.
- A **fake college schedule** — classes "always at 9pm" — kept up for **more than
  eighteen months**. It won him the busy, high-tip shifts, got him out of closing
  up, and covered an affair with a coworker.
- A territory: sole custody of the dying outdoor shed section, and the cash tips
  that passed through it.

That last detail is what stops this from being a vague idea that explains
everything. **The hidden goal is never the job's own goal, and it usually cuts
against it.** If any small private satisfaction counted, the rule would predict
nothing at all.

The golf resort is the same shape with no dishonesty in it. The employer's job
description was "guest golf-round assistance, pace-of-play management, equipment
logistics." What Dan actually tracked and celebrated was a ladder his employer
never set and never measured: being called up to work with "the real loopers,"
the 4:30 AM departures, the first round carrying two bags at once, and a
transfer to Scottsdale he pitched directly to the head of that caddie programme.
Forty-three months of hard physical work, held up by a private scoreboard.

## The cases that test it

**The gas station is the test case — and it is a weaker test than it looks.**
About one month in early 2026, with no private goal anywhere in the record of
it. It ended when the job made its first genuinely assigned demand: pay a **$50**
till shortage out of his own pocket and sign a write-up. He refused both, his
hours dropped from **36 to 7** the same day, and it was over.

But there are now **two accounts of why that job ended, and they don't match.**
In a message on 2026-08-11 Dan said something quite different: *"literally the
only thing I did was go home 30 minutes before my shift was supposed to end
after I called my manager and made sure it was okay."* And the ending was more
formal than this page once recorded — a posted sign reading **"NO HIRE: Daniel
Frank"** in two places. Dan found it arbitrary enough to say it made him
*"wonder if I am going crazy."*

That matters, because a test case has to be clean. If an employer decision ended
the job, then the short stay is partly explained by something other than the
thing being measured. The safe version survives: no private goal was installed,
and it didn't last. The specific story about the till shortage is now one of two
competing accounts and shouldn't be stated as settled.

One part of the reversal does support the rule. The same managers later told him
to reapply, and he did. A job he'll happily re-enter for money but never sticks
at is exactly the predicted shape — the appetite for wages is constant, the
staying power isn't.

**Now compare the restaurant.** For three months — June, July and August 2024 —
his employer cut his hours in a pattern he read at the time as an attempt to
force him to quit. He didn't quit. Three months of deliberately worsening
conditions did not end a 41-month job. **Losing the territory did.** Once the
finished indoor dining room opened, demand for the outdoor tables died and the
shed was already obsolete — *"then also the city made places tear down sheds for
winter, so that was that."* The private goal decayed and was then formally
removed, and he went with it.

**The courier work isolates the variable best.** An app that pings orders to a
phone offers nothing to hide a goal inside — no territory, no ladder, nobody to
negotiate with, no supervision to dodge. The pattern is exactly what the rule
predicts: busy April to July 2019, almost nothing from August through November,
scattered orders in March 2020, one last burst in April 2020. Twelve months
available, no real tenure. He wanted the money the whole time. There was just
nothing to attach the engine to.

**The 2015 arrest is the hardest case.** A court handed him a six-month
probation programme — a goal set by someone else, with real penalties for
failure. The records document him *getting* that deal in dense, dated detail:
hiring a lawyer, December trips to the barracks to replace lost paperwork, a
January visit to the programme office, a February 17 hearing, the fines cleared.
**Five separate dated events across ten weeks.**

The six months of actually complying are on the record too, but only as a single
sentence he offered a decade later: he "completed that without incident." So the
asymmetry isn't that one is missing. It's density. **The getting is recorded as
it happens, five times over; the serving is recorded once, looking back.** Worth
noting what he was up against: the prosecutor's office had a blanket policy
against offering that programme for cocaine charges at all, residue or not.

## What this changes

The older idea isn't overturned, it's aimed wrong. Its central point — that
nobody else can point Dan's drive at a target — holds up fine. What fails is the
conclusion drawn from it: that assigned work therefore can't get sustained
effort. It can. It gets it through a private goal smuggled inside. **An
assignment is a container.** The question is never whether someone else set the
goal, but whether Dan found or installed something of his own in there.

Two things follow.

**How long an assigned commitment lasts tells you nothing on its own.** The
41-month job and the 1-month job differ by a factor of forty, and the difference
isn't pay, difficulty, or how the boss behaved. It's whether a private goal was
present.

**And wanting something yourself is necessary but not enough.** The strong
version of the older claim was that a self-chosen goal runs to completion almost
regardless of cost. His own project record kills that. Three projects — a memory
system, a model-evaluation study, and a series of AI video essays — were entirely
self-chosen, assigned by nobody, and **none of them finished.** The engine needs
a self-set goal to start. Having one is no guarantee it runs.

## Why an assignment doesn't count until he rewrites it

His cognitive profile offers a mechanism. He scores 96th percentile on internal
logical analysis, and that style treats something as true only when it survives
being taken apart and rebuilt internally — never because an authority said so.

An assigned goal shows up pre-labelled as *somebody else's conclusion*. It hasn't
passed that internal test yet, so it isn't real to him. A tip split he argued
into existence, a status ladder he kept score on himself, a territory he claimed
and named — each of those is the same work re-derived from scratch until he owns
it. At that point it behaves like anything else he concluded himself: defended
far past what the official role would justify.

**One honest complication.** This page leans on the idea that a self-chosen goal
recruits a real completion engine. His personality measurements read the very
same behaviour — 96th percentile impulsiveness — as a **brake failure** instead:
not an engine that works, but an inability to stop once locked on. Both readings
fit every row in the table above equally well. A private goal that produces 41
months looks identical either way. This page's evidence genuinely cannot decide
between them, and the wording throughout ("the drive," "the engine") has been
quietly taking one side. That's now said out loud rather than assumed.

## Lasting a long time and finding it worthwhile are different things

This page measures how long commitments last. It says nothing about whether any
of them were good for him — and the records say those two things move
independently.

The restaurant ran 41 months and is named as the origin of his "job entropy"
cycle: long service, no satisfaction reported. The six-month stretch caring for
his dying great-grandmother is **a seventh of that length**, and it is the only
commitment anywhere in **106,629 sent messages** that he reports back as having
gone well: *"we had a lot of fun. got good closure and finally did something in
my life that wasn't completely selfish"* (2018-04-06).

What that one had, and the jobs didn't, is **an ending.** The jobs are
open-ended containers that run until the private goal is destroyed from outside
or was never installed. None of them ever finishes. The caregiving was bounded by
a death, and he closed it himself.

So these are two separate rules that overlap exactly once. One explains how long
he lasts inside somebody else's structure. The other explains what feels
satisfying — closing something with a findable edge. Usually they describe
different things: a job has no edge to find, and a finishable project has no
employer to hide inside. The caregiving was unusual on both counts at once, and
that coincidence is why it stands alone in the record.

## Why none of it added up to anything

There's a harder consequence. **A goal hidden inside somebody else's job cannot
be taken with you.** The 41 months and the 43 months were real work that was
never structurally his at any point. When the shed came down in August 2024, the
goal went with it and nothing accumulated.

That reframes an obvious question about his life — why thirty-seven years of
genuinely high output has produced no qualification, no savings and nothing he
can point to and claim. What's missing isn't drive; this page shows plenty of
that. What's missing is **somewhere of his own to put it** — a container he owns,
so a goal doesn't need a host to hide in. The one candidate in the record is his
music production identity: **thirteen years, one continuous name since 2016** —
longer than any job he's held. Nobody can test it, because no output figures for
it exist anywhere.

## What would prove this wrong

- **A long job with no private goal installed early on.** The rule says every job
  will show either a hidden goal or an escape hatch put in during the first few
  weeks — or else it will be short. A long one with neither breaks this page
  outright. The named test is the roughly **eleven-month** gap in work after
  August 2024 and the job he described as his "first time working since june
  2024." That job is barely documented, and whichever way it turns out, it counts
  as evidence.
- **A job that ended because conditions got worse rather than because the private
  goal was destroyed.** The restaurant is the worked example of the opposite. Any
  future job loss can be checked against which happened first.
- **Sustained, costly obedience to an instruction he never adopted as his own.**
  Assigned obligations with nothing to hide inside should show the *getting*
  documented and the *doing* undocumented, the way the court programme does. The
  sharpest test available is his fifteen-year daily medication regimen. It began
  as a prescription, and the records document the supply side of it in enormous
  detail — which this page reads as him having converted it into his own goal
  early, since he described it in his own words as the thing that *"helped me
  finally get out of that world."* If a source ever shows him paying a real price,
  for years, to follow a medical instruction he never made his own, the rule
  has to narrow again.

## What we still don't know

- **The golf resort's private goal is inferred, not stated.** It comes from what
  Dan chose to write down, not from him ever saying that was the point. It's the
  weakest of the three positive cases — a status ladder is exactly the sort of
  thing a person invents afterwards to explain why they stayed.
- **The gas station is scored on missing evidence.** "No private goal" rests on
  not finding one in a single month of records. An escape hatch that was
  installed and simply never used would look exactly the same.
- **Every job before 2015 is almost entirely undocumented.** The rule is tested
  against four commitments, not against a working life.
- **Nobody knows how he picks.** It's established that he installs an escape
  hatch deliberately and says so himself. But nothing in the records shows him
  choosing *which* goal to embed, or what he does in a role that offers several.
