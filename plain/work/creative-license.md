# Creative License (NYC, 2011–2012)

**The short version.** A small celebrity-talent and music-brokering company in the
West Village where Dan worked for ten months in 2011 and 2012 — and whose ending
ran through an airfare citation, a payroll dispute, a whistleblower disclosure,
a notarized act of retaliation, and a LinkedIn threat, before Dan filed a
counter-notice and kept his boss's email address for the better part of a
decade. The company president, Kevin McKiernan, eventually tried to erase Dan's
professional identity from LinkedIn using notarized false statements. Dan fought
back. The two were still on each other's contact lists years later. It is the
most fully documented workplace conflict in the entire archive.

## The job itself

Creative License brokered licensed music and celebrity talent — its one recovered
client pitch is a Walmart RFP for "3rd Party Negotiator — Licensed Music and
Celebrity Talent," not the generic production services earlier guesses suggested.
Dan's title was **"Special Projects Coordinator"** — his résumé later listed
"Executive Assistant / Office Manager," but a contemporaneous LinkedIn notice
from 2012 independently uses "Special Projects Coordinator," which settles which
title is real.

Day-to-day, the job was ordinary office work: covering for migraines and doctor's
appointments (a recurring pattern — July, September, October, November 2011,
January 2012), coordinating client relationships with **Walmart** and **Molson
Coors**, helping prep a colleague for speaking appearances, and once tracking
down a departed employee to recover company property — the same tactic later
turned on Dan himself.

In May 2011, the company's Director of Business Affairs, Katherine Palakovich,
sent Dan his own non-disclosure and non-compete agreement to sign. Dan forwarded
it to his father Rick the same day with a note: "Thought you might want to take
a look at this." Rick was consulted on employment paperwork from the very start,
not only at the acrimonious end.

## How the ending unfolded, in four acts

**Act zero — the airfare citation.** Per Dan's own later account, what he cited
at the time as his reason for quitting was a "small fraud with airplane tickets" —
some billing irregularity in Kevin's travel expenses that Dan, in a role with
real "oversight of office and employee expenses" responsibility, was positioned
to notice. Dan's own retrospective framing is self-doubting: he used it as "the
'legal ground' to detach" because a personality complaint would have been
dismissed as him being "sensitive," while a "financial anomaly" made the
departure "an objective failure" on the company's part rather than his own. He
explicitly wonders, years later, whether he "blew it up into a much much bigger
thing" or was simply desensitized to normal industry practice. The specific
mechanism of the alleged fraud is not independently verified; only the fact that
Dan cited it is confirmed, by his own primary testimony inside a later session.

**Act one — the payroll dispute.** Dan's last day was on or before **February
15, 2012** — the date of his first email flagging that his final paycheck had a
**$104 charge for a MetroCard he says he never requested or received.** The
check also arrived unsigned. Over the next six weeks Dan and the company's
accounting officer, Marty Jackson, went back and forth over the missing
signature, the disputed charge, and a W-2 that didn't arrive on schedule. Marty's
position was that Dan needed to have opted out of the automatic MetroCard program
"in writing before you quit," which Dan disputed ever being told. On February 27,
after Dan pushed back on Marty's math, Marty wrote the line that anchors this
whole episode:

> "It's a trust issue. We don't trust you. Please do not forget the keys or any
> other CL property you have."

Dan escalated by filing complaints with the **IRS and the NYS Department of
Labor**, and CC'd his father Rick throughout. Rick functioned as tactical coach
rather than authority figure: "You sound so desperate. That is what kevin wants.
We'll talk about a strategy for this." By April 9, Dan closed the dispute out
directly with Marty — conceding the $104 to end it, and explicitly separating
the accountant from the president: "I didn't mean to give the impression that my
hostility was directed at you personally... I don't believe that your intention
was to create a volatile or unpleasant situation." The distinction between the
functionary and the principal is Dan's own, made in real time.

**Act two — the catharsis.** Two months after leaving, Dan sent a former
coworker, Rachel Rauch, a one-line email — "Enjoy!!!" — with an attachment
titled `BEST OF KTM VOICEMAILS.mp3`, a compiled recording of McKiernan's
"erratic, high-volatility communication style." The relationship's end produced
an archive, not just a grievance.

**Act three — the whistleblower disclosure.** Unprompted, months after the
payroll dispute closed, Dan wrote to another former contact, Renae Holland, to
explain something that had been bothering him: in December 2011, while he was
still employed, Kevin had used Dan's own email and LinkedIn account to send
recommendation-removal requests to former employees — Renae among them. Dan's
own words:

> "I (my account, rather) was acting as a channel for Kevin to act out his most
> immature fantasies and I'm glad I can say that I didn't even help write those
> emails... The more alarming aspect of this was the employment contracts that
> Kevin had me send to the two former interns. These documents had been altered
> before they were sent and a clause was added that prohibited recommendations
> without CEO approval... I think that the two interns should be aware of this
> for their own legal protection and because it's just disgustingly wrong."

Renae's reply corroborates independently, from her own tenure at the company:
"in my time there I was Kevin falsify more documents that I can count — I know
what he is capable of." This is the real substance behind the garbled allegation
of "altered intern contracts" — a specific, named mechanism (a CEO-approval gag
clause quietly inserted into departing interns' paperwork), not a vague charge.

**Act four — the retaliation.** A month after the whistleblower email, Kevin
filed a formal LinkedIn intellectual-property complaint against Dan's profile,
backed by **two notarized documents asserting Dan had never been a Creative
License employee and had fabricated his job title** — notarized by Katherine
Palakovich, using her own notary commission on behalf of her employer against a
former coworker. Dan explicitly flagged this as a professional-responsibility
violation in his reply to her, citing the Notary Public Code. LinkedIn threatened
to terminate Dan's account within three business days. Dan filed a counter-
notice, offering "paystubs, tax documentation, or any other official
documentation," and named the retaliation motive directly: "Mr. McKiernan has and
continues to attempt to smear my name because of my actions as a whistleblower
while under his employ." No resolution email was located — whether LinkedIn
sided with Dan is unconfirmed, though his profile clearly survived.

## The postscript

Despite all of the above, Dan kept Kevin McKiernan's contact information for
close to a decade (Facebook address-book snapshots from 2016 through 2021 still
carry `kevin@creativelicense.com`), and in December 2014 Katherine Palakovich —
by then leaving the company herself — bcc'd Dan on a mass "Ex-Creative License
Happy Hour" invitation addressed to "all my favorite ex (and current) CL
employees." Two and a half years after a conflict serious enough to involve the
IRS, the NYS Department of Labor, and a notarized false statement, Dan was still
on the alumni list. Neither fact cancels the other; both are true.

By February 2012, Dan was also already job-hunting in the ordinary sense —
applying to EA and Office Manager roles across Manhattan and reaching out to
firms like the Bond Street Group. That matters for how the "principled
whistleblower exit" reading should be held: it does not contradict anything
documented above, but it means the departure was probably also an ordinary job
change in progress, not purely a dramatic break — consistent with Dan's own
later self-doubt about whether the airfare complaint was proportionate to what
actually happened.

## What this connects to

The corpus's own naming identifies this job and a later one — BFS Foods — as the
first and most recent instances of one shape, fourteen years apart: a trusted
vertical figure's undocumented financial claim met with exit and documentation
rather than confrontation. It is also the best-documented instance of the
authority-skepticism pattern below the BFS case: a company's own accounting
officer telling Dan in writing "we don't trust you," followed four months later
by its president backing a false, notarized claim to get Dan's professional
identity erased from LinkedIn — met with a filed IRS/Dept-of-Labor complaint
and a successful counter-notice rather than capitulation.

## What we still don't know

- **The specific mechanism behind the airfare citation** — what Dan actually
  found in the travel-expense records, and whether it involved Walmart or
  Molson Coors billing — is unverified; only the fact that he cited it is
  confirmed.
- **What specifically happened to Renae Holland** — her phrasing implies she
  was herself a target of some Kevin McKiernan campaign, but the substance
  wasn't recovered.
- **The exact text of the notarized documents** Kevin submitted to LinkedIn was
  not extracted.
- **Whether LinkedIn ultimately ruled for or against Dan** is unconfirmed.
- **Ishlab Studios**, the job immediately preceding this one, still has no
  page of its own in the available record.
